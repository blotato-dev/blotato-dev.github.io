Blotato Review (2026): Pricing, Limits and What It Does

Blotato is a social media API plus MCP server, sold on the idea that your agent posts for you instead of you opening nine apps.

Bottom line firstThis is a good fit if you already run an agent or an automation stack and want publishing to be one more tool call rather than a browser tab. It is a poor fit if nobody on your team has ever written an n8n scenario or configured an MCP server, because the interface you are paying for is machine-shaped by design. People who want a calendar, a queue and a human to press approve are describing Supapush, not this. The caveat is dependency: routing distribution through an agent means an agent failure becomes a publishing failure.

The actual pitch, in plain terms

The site describes a unified API and MCP server that can post, schedule, reply to comments, manage DMs and track analytics across nine or more social platforms without writing code. The listed networks are X, LinkedIn, Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Threads, Bluesky and Pinterest, and the headline example is a single create_post call fanning out to all of them. On the client side it names Claude, Claude Code, Cowork, ChatGPT, Cursor, Codex and others, plus n8n, Make and plain REST for people who prefer their automation without a chat window. Read the whole thing as one sentence: it is the OAuth and per-platform SDK problem, solved once, exposed as tools an agent can call.

What the published numbers do and do not tell you

The home page carries a starter price of 29 dollars a month, described as flat pricing with unlimited posts and 20 connected accounts, a seven-day free trial and cancel-anytime terms. Elsewhere it states 1,800 requests per hour, 667 thousand or more posts published last month, 10,000 or more business owners on the platform and an eight-in-one framing against separate subscriptions. Those last few are marketing figures from the vendor and prove adoption rather than fit. The two numbers to actually hold on to are the request ceiling and the account count, because those are the ones that decide whether your use case fits the entry plan. Check both against the official pricing page before committing a workflow to it.

Comments, DMs and the automation nobody mentions

Publishing is the boring half. The more interesting part of the feature list is the inbox: listing comments and conversations, reading a thread and replying or sending DMs without a human opening the app, plus keyword-triggered DM automations where somebody comments a word and gets a message with a link. The site notes that DM automation part covers Instagram and Facebook, with its own triggered, completed and failed reporting. That is a real funnel primitive rather than a demo feature. It is also the half that needs the most care, because an agent that replies to strangers on your behalf will eventually reply to something you would not have.

Scheduling that an agent can reason about

You can post now, post at a timestamp, or ask for the next open slot and drop the post into it, with recurring weekly slots configurable per platform and per account. That third option is the one worth noticing, because it is written for a caller that does not have a calendar in front of it. A human picks Tuesday at ten because they can see the week. An agent asks what is free and takes it. If your content pipeline produces things in bursts and you want them spread out without micromanagement, that is a genuinely different model from a traditional scheduler, and it is the clearest argument for this product over a conventional one.

Who should use Supapush instead

If the person doing the posting is a marketer rather than an engineer, an API-first product creates work instead of removing it. Nothing to configure, no MCP client, no failed tool call to debug at eight in the morning. The trade is control versus automation. If you want a machine to publish, this review's subject is built for that. If you want to see the week laid out and approve what goes out, take the composer.

A sensible first week

  1. Connect two accounts, not twenty
    Start with the two networks that matter most to you. Connecting everything on day one turns your first mistake into nine public mistakes across nine audiences.
  2. Publish one post by hand
    Make a single call yourself, from the client you intend to use, before any agent touches it. You want to see exactly what the request and response look like when nothing is wrapping them.
  3. Put a human in the loop
    Have the agent draft and queue rather than publish immediately for the first fortnight. Review the queue each morning. You will catch tone problems that no prompt would have prevented.
  4. Watch the request ceiling
    The site states 1,800 requests per hour. Log your own usage for a week and compare, especially if the agent polls comments, before you build anything that assumes unlimited calls.

FAQ

What does Blotato actually do?

It exposes social publishing as an API and an MCP server, so an agent or an automation platform can create posts, schedule them, read and reply to comments, handle DMs and pull analytics across the nine or more networks named on its home page, rather than a person logging into each app.

How much does Blotato cost?

The home page advertises a starter plan at 29 dollars a month with flat pricing, unlimited posts and 20 connected accounts, alongside a seven-day free trial and cancel-anytime terms. Plan structures change, so read the official pricing page rather than trusting any figure quoted second hand.

Do I need to write code?

The site says no code is required and supports n8n, Make and MCP clients as well as plain REST. In practice you still need somebody comfortable configuring an automation or an MCP server. That is a lower bar than writing a parser, but it is not zero.

Which platforms are supported?

X, LinkedIn, Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Threads, Bluesky and Pinterest are named directly on the home page, described as nine or more platforms behind one call. Feature depth varies by network, and the keyword DM automation is noted as covering Instagram and Facebook.

Is an agent-driven setup risky for a brand account?

It carries a real failure mode: automated replies and DMs go out without anyone reading them first. Keep a human approving the queue while you learn the tool, and restrict automated replies to narrow keyword triggers rather than open-ended conversation.

What if I just want a calendar and a queue?

Then a composer suits you better than an API. Supapush schedules across ten networks with a queue, a calendar and analytics in one browser view, with no MCP client to configure. Same publishing job, opposite assumption about who is driving.
